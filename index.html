I'm a final-year Mathematics and Scientific Computing student at IIIT Gwalior working on machine learning research. Most of my work circles one question: when a model reports a result, how do we know the result means what we think it means? That's taken me through evaluating LLM hallucination in low-resource languages, building bias annotation datasets for Indian media, and — currently — predicting neural network activations analytically from weights alone, without ever running the network. I care about the gap between what a benchmark measures and what actually happens in deployment, and I like problems where the honest answer requires ruling things out rather than piling up evidence for one story. I've shipped production ML systems in fintech and healthcare, and I'm heading toward research in evaluation methodology and model internals.

ARC Prize · WhestBench Phase 2

ARC White-Box Estimation Challenge

Research Applied Math

Predicting the expected post-ReLU activations of randomly-initialised ReLU MLPs analytically from weights alone, without sampling, under a hard FLOP budget. The final estimator scores 1.31e-07 on 50 held-out MLPs — 3.1× better than the covariance-propagation baseline and 9.0× better than Monte Carlo, at 2.36% of budget.

Python flopscope NumPy Hermite/Mehler
GitHub

Given the weight matrices of a He-initialised, bias-free ReLU MLP (width 1024, depth 16), the task is to predict the expected post-ReLU activation of every neuron under Gaussian input — without ever running the network. Score is final-layer MSE multiplied by the fraction of a 241 FLOP budget consumed, floored at 10%.

The Mehler closure. The shipped covariance-propagation baseline closes the post-ReLU covariance with a “gain” rule. I showed that rule is precisely the k=1 term of the Mehler expansion of Cov(ReLU(zi), ReLU(zj)), derived the general Hermite coefficient for k ≥ 2, and carried the series further — verified against brute-force sampling. It works, but it is small: about 5% MSE reduction, converging by order 2. So the closure was never the main thing wrong.

Localising the error by oracle injection. Supplying the true per-layer mean improved MSE 19.7×, while supplying the true covariance made it worse (0.65×). All the remaining error lived in the mean path, and the covariance was already good enough. The residual turned out to be almost purely multiplicative — covariance propagation over-predicts by roughly 0.16% — and a greedy per-layer affine non-Gaussianity fit captures 65% of it.

Documented negative results. These are a feature of the work, not a caveat. An Edgeworth κ₄ correction failed even when handed an oracle kurtosis (0.89×, worse) — ReLU's kink breaks the expansion, which depends on derivatives of the density at exactly that point. Fitting all 16 layer corrections simultaneously diverged (300× worse), because each layer's residual already contains the propagated effect of earlier errors. Correcting the mean without re-deriving the variance from the uncorrected mean made things worse (0.61×) — a two-line ordering detail worth 4.8×. And Monte Carlo could not supply the constants: sampling noise came out 5× larger than the between-MLP signal it was trying to measure.

Honest caveat, stated in the repo. The winning submission ships 32 fitted constants (a per-layer pair) measured on the public suite rather than derived from first principles. Cross-fitting on either half of the suite agrees to within 1%, held-out R² matches in-sample R², and per-MLP oracle coefficients are only ~2% better — but the submission still guards on shape and degrades gracefully to plain Mehler covariance propagation (3.83e-07) rather than corrupting on a different configuration.

Runner-Up NHA AB PM-JAY · IISc Bangalore

Auto-Adjudication for Insurance-Based Clinical Report OCR and Reasoning

Vision-Language Healthcare Team project

A two-pass vision-language pipeline that classifies insurance claim document pages and extracts clinical evidence against STG rules for four PM-JAY package codes. Runner-Up out of 40 teams, with mandatory F1 0.915 and clinical F1 0.791.

Gemma 3 12B PyMuPDF Python
GitHub

Built for the National Health Authority's auto-adjudication problem statement at IISc Bangalore: given a bundle of scanned claim documents, decide which pages are which document type, and extract the clinical evidence that a package code's Standard Treatment Guidelines actually require. The pipeline covers four package codes — MG064A (severe anemia), SG039C (cholecystectomy), MG006A (enteric fever) and SB039A (total knee replacement).

Two passes, cached. The first pass runs per-page document-type classification and entity extraction; the second runs a per-package clinical-field pass against the STG rules. Every Gemma call is disk-cached, so re-runs cost zero fresh tokens — total usage stayed at 11% of the 25.5M token budget across the whole event.

What the ablations showed. Fusing keyword evidence and VLM evidence by intersection beat either signal alone, lifting clinical F1 from 0.757 to 0.791 — the model's false positives and the keyword matcher's false positives were largely disjoint, so requiring agreement cut them without much recall cost. Separately, ablating four document-ranking schemes moved rank score from 0.008 to 0.107.

Final scored submission: mandatory F1 0.9154, clinical F1 0.7909, extra F1 0.7684, rank score 0.1066 — final score 0.7662, second of 40 teams against a held-out organizer evaluator.

Meta × PyTorch × Hugging Face Hackathon

QuantumScribe

RL Quantum Collaboration with Ronit Raj

An LLM decoder for quantum error correction: a 3B-parameter model learns to decode surface-code syndromes, trained with SFT then GRPO against a Stim physics simulator. Reaches 96.4% logical correction on a free Colab T4 in about three hours.

Qwen2.5-3B GRPO LoRA Stim FastAPI
GitHub

Quantum error correction decoding is normally a job for specialised classical decoders like minimum-weight perfect matching. QuantumScribe asks whether a general-purpose language model can learn the same mapping — reading a surface-code syndrome and writing out the correction — and does it on hardware anyone can get for free.

Training. Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct is fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 16), first through 50 steps of supervised fine-tuning and then 1,500 steps of GRPO. Crucially the reward is verifiable physics, not human preference: five independent components covering logical correction, syndrome consistency, Hamming overlap with the true correction, output format compliance, and beat-rate against the PyMatching baseline.

Results across the two stages. Logical correction went from 85.0% after SFT to 96.4% after GRPO; format compliance from 26.3% to 100%; Hamming overlap from 0.645 to 0.840. The format jump is the clearest evidence that GRPO was doing real work — a verifiable reward on output structure fixed something SFT alone had not.

Infrastructure. The environment is an OpenEnv-compliant FastAPI server wrapping the Stim simulator and a PyMatching reference decoder, so training and evaluation both run against actual physics rather than a cached dataset. The repo ships a Dockerfile, a Gradio demo, trained weights and notebooks under an MIT license.

OpenEnv · RL Environment

Inference Gym

RL AI Infrastructure Collaboration with Ronit Raj

An OpenEnv-compliant RL environment that treats LLM inference serving as a control problem — batching, KV-cache budget, speculative decoding depth, quantization and routing — under latency, memory and cost constraints. A trained PPO policy beats hand-tuned serving heuristics on all three tasks.

Python PPO OpenEnv FastAPI Docker
GitHub

LLMServeEnv models the decision an inference server actually faces: with request traffic changing underneath you, how do you set batching and resource policy? The agent controls a typed action — batch_cap (1–512), kv_budget_fraction (0.1–1.0), speculation_depth (0–8), a quantization tier (FP16/INT8/INT4), whether to split prefill from decode, and whether to route by priority.

Observations and reward. The environment reports queue depth, p50/p99 TTFT and inter-token latency, throughput, memory use, estimated cost, SLO compliance, speculation acceptance rate and eviction events. Reward is shaped at every step rather than only at episode end, combining throughput, SLO compliance, memory pressure and cost. The simulator is CPU-only and deterministic under a fixed seed, so grading is reproducible.

Three graded tasks. static_workload (stable rate, short prompts), bursty_workload (bursty arrivals, queue volatility) and adversarial_multitenant (mixed prompt lengths, traffic spikes, priority pressure). The design goal was that hand-coded heuristics should not be able to solve it optimally — the workloads are non-stationary and the resource trade-offs are interdependent.

Benchmarks. Across the three tasks, random policies score roughly 0.05 / 0.03 / 0.02 and a heuristic stack modelled on Orca, vLLM and Decima scores 0.30 / 0.25 / 0.20. A trained PPO policy reaches 0.55 / 0.48 / 0.38. The repo ships the full OpenEnv runtime surface (/reset, /step, /tasks, /grader, /baseline, /web), a Docker build and a pre-submission check script.

AISE Hackathon · Phase 2

Neural Operator Based PM2.5 Forecasting

Scientific ML Forecasting Collaboration with Ronit Raj

Spatio-temporal air-quality forecasting: predicting PM2.5 concentration 16 hours ahead from a 10-hour lookback on a 140×124 grid, using a Fourier Neural Operator baseline and a hybrid FNO + ConvLSTM ensemble.

PyTorch FNO ConvLSTM NumPy
GitHub

The forecasting target is a 140×124 spatial grid: given 10 hours of lookback across 29 channels (16 raw, 13 derived), predict PM2.5 for the next 16 hours. The hard part is not average accuracy — it is the pollution episodes, the spikes that matter most and occur least often.

Two architectures. The baseline is an FNO2D — Fourier neural operator blocks with spectral convolution, SE and CBAM attention, and a UNet-style decoder. The later experiment is a HybridFNOConvLSTMEnsemble: an FNO branch for global spatial structure alongside a three-layer ConvLSTM branch for temporal evolution, combined by an adaptive gated fusion with cross-branch interaction, plus a per-pixel episode-probability head that blends the two.

Training against the episode problem. The loss is an episode-weighted SMAPE with an auxiliary BCE term for episode classification, and training uses a curriculum that escalates the episode weight α from 1.0 to 5.0 to 8.0 across epochs — so the model learns the general field first and is pushed onto the spikes later. Validation tracks global SMAPE, episode SMAPE and episode correlation separately, since a model can look good on the first and fail on the other two.

Special Mention AMD AI Hackathon · AMD, Unsloth, IIT Delhi

Minesweeper RL

RL LLM Fine-tuning Collaboration with Ronit Raj

A GRPO-trained language-model agent that plays Minesweeper, emitting moves as structured JSON. Placed 4th of 60 teams with a Special Mention at the AMD AI Hackathon.

GRPO LoRA Qwen Unsloth
GitHub

Minesweeper is a useful RL testbed for language models because the reasoning is genuinely deductive but the action space is tiny, so the whole difficulty sits in whether the model can read a board state and commit to a defensible move in a parseable format.

Training setup. The agent is trained with GRPO using LoRA (rank 32) at a 5e-6 learning rate, generating 24 candidates per group for reward diversity, and capped at 128 new tokens per move to force concise JSON output rather than rambling chain-of-thought.

Reward shaping. The reward is deliberately format-dominant — 60% output format, 35% move correctness, 5% efficiency. Early on, an agent that reasons well but emits unparseable output scores zero, so format has to be learned first before correctness can be rewarded at all.

Exploration schedule. Two scheduling tricks carried the run: temperature annealing from 1.2 down to 0.7 across the first 70% of training, and a curriculum over board sizes shaped as a bell curve, with mid-range boards (8–20 cells) taking about 35% of the training weight. Prompts are tiered across four difficulty levels, from minimal hints on tiny boards to comprehensive structural guidance on boards up to 50 cells.
